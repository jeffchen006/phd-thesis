\section{Motivation}

Software systems are increasingly used to manage real-world
assets of significant value. Software is built to store, manage,
and operate on digital assets, including 
bank accounts, credentials, cryptocurrencies, and classified blueprints.
This trend is large and
accelerating: the IMF's 2025 Financial 
Access Survey reports that
digital transactions, including mobile 
money and mobile and internet
banking, have surged in emerging and 
developing economies, rising
from 55 transactions per adult in 2017 to 251 per adult by
2024~\cite{imf2025fas}. As more value 
moves into software, a single
defect or compromise can translate directly into 
real-world harm.

Not only has software itself changed, but so 
has the way
software is built. Despite well-known
limitations of their black-box nature,
it is by now an
undeniable fact that developers 
routinely rely on large language models (LLMs),
and other AI-assisted coding
tools to help implement
software~\cite{liang2024largescale, 
barke2023grounded}.
These 
tools dramatically lower the
barrier to development: as long as a 
need can be described in natural
language, the tool can try its best to 
help produce a 
corresponding implementation.
This opens software development to a far wider audience, including
people with little formal programming 
background. Tools such as Claude
Code~\cite{anthropic_claude_code}, Codex~\cite{openai_codex_2026}, and
OpenCode~\cite{opencode_2026} are now widely 
used in practice. 


Not only the way software is built, but also the way it is used has changed.
Traditionally,
software was designed with a specific expected 
usage in mind: developers of software
for launching a rocket could assume it 
would be operated by trained
astronauts, and developers of an iOS application 
could assume it would be used by a human tapping on an
iPhone screen. 
That assumption is increasingly untenable. 
Autonomous
agents, including other software or AI agents
acting on behalf of humans, now interact with software freely, 
often
in ways its original developers never anticipated. 
On social platforms,
automated bots already rival or exceed human activity: by 2024,
bots generated roughly half of all internet traffic, 
surpassing humans
for the first time, and about a fifth of that traffic was classified as
malicious~\cite{imperva2024badbot}. 
The classical defenses no longer
hold either; a 2024 study solved Google's reCAPTCHAv2 image challenges
with a 100\% success rate, up from 68--71\% for prior
methods~\cite{plesner2024recaptcha}. 
Autonomous
agents are also used to
manufacture reputation: freshly created accounts 
inflate the apparent
popularity of open-source projects, and one 2024 measurement detected
6 million fake GitHub stars whose volume surged sharply that
year~\cite{he2024fakestars}. 
The incentives grow sharper when money is
at stake. In so-called \emph{airdrops}, where blockchain projects
distribute tokens to reward active users, professional users 
deploy hundreds of
wallets, phones, and accounts to automatically 
impersonate many independent
participants and create fake activity to claim more rewards; 
for its June 2024 ZRO distribution, LayerZero alone
flagged more than 800{,}000 wallets (about 13\% of those
eligible) as such Sybil farmers~\cite{layerzero2024airdrop}.



These two shifts, in how software is built and in how it is
used, bring clear convenience, but also new risks
beyond those of the traditional software-development workflow.

Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} further clarifies the
new risks introduced on top of the existing software development
lifecycle. First, in the software development stage, not 
only do humans
make mistakes in the limited number of lines they write themselves, 
but
errors can also arise in the large number of lines of code generated by
AI-assisted coding tools. Such a large volume of code makes it
difficult even for expert developers to review, let alone the new
non-technical programmers who have not received sufficient software
training. Section~\ref{subsec:example1} later presents a
real-world case in which ChatGPT wrongly used a 
phishing scam URL in a code snippet, and
sent the user's private key to the scam server,
causing a loss of more than \$2,000 USD,
even though the user's prompts were
typical developer-style requests.


Second, in the software deployment stage, the tasks of
monitoring usage, maintaining the system, and patching it accordingly
face an unprecedented challenge: alongside human users, a growing number
of autonomous agents now invoke the software. Traditional metrics such
as click rates and day--night usage patterns no longer apply. A human
may use social media only while awake, following a natural biological
cycle, whereas an autonomous agent can access the software at any time
and at a far larger scale. Section~\ref{subsec:example3} later presents
a real-world case in which an attacker exploited a blockchain
application in a way dramatically different from every other user,
causing millions of dollars in losses even though the software itself
had been reviewed by professional auditors.

Because of these shifts, we are already witnessing rapidly
growing security threats to software. Cyberattacks are not a new
problem. The IMF reports that the financial sector has lost \$12
billion over the past two decades to more than 20{,}000 such
incidents~\cite{financemagnates2024imf}. However, the two changes
above sharply amplify the threat. On the development side,
AI-generated code has already caused direct financial loss. In 
2026, a faulty price-oracle update in a pull request co-authored by an
AI coding model mispriced 
collateral on the Moonwell lending protocol,
leading to roughly \$1.78 million in losses~\cite{moonwell2026exploit} (detailed
in Section~\ref{subsec:example2}).

On the usage side, autonomous agents have themselves been turned into
attack tools. A state-sponsored group weaponized an AI 
coding agent to
run a largely autonomous cyber-espionage campaign against around
thirty organizations before the provider detected the activity and
disabled the accounts~\cite{anthropic2025espionage}. 
These attacks
compound an already worsening landscape. In 2025, a record year for
cryptocurrency theft, attackers stole more than \$3 billion in digital
assets~\cite{chainalysis2025crypto}.

It is therefore increasingly important to protect what we call
\emph{high-stakes software}. Informally, a high-stakes software
system is durable software whose purpose is to protect
and operate on critical assets such as digital assets,
user credentials, and classified documents. 
Such systems naturally
attract attackers, and we believe they 
are the most valuable to
defend. In contrast, disposable, single-use programs that are written
and used only
once fall outside our scope. 

This thesis focuses on securing high-stakes software against the
two untrusted factors introduced above: AI-assisted coding tools at
development time, and autonomous agents at deployment time. 
Following the
lifecycle of such systems, we examine, at each stage, how these factors
can be exploited to compromise the assets the software guards, and we
build defenses that contain this risk both before and after
deployment.



\subsection{High-Stakes Software}
\label{subsec:high-stakes-software}

A software system is \emph{high-stakes} when its failure can cause real
harm outside the software itself, by exposing or destroying money, secrets,
credentials, personal data, or critical infrastructure. What makes software
high-stakes is the consequence of its failure, 
not the size of its code: a short
script handed a private key is high-stakes, while 
a large text parser whose
mistakes are merely an annoyance is not. 
This dissertation focuses on durable
systems entrusted with such assets. 
The most direct \emph{stakes} we
consider are \emph{cryptocurrencies}: stealing them 
causes an immediate, direct,
and
measurable financial loss, unlike the theft of credentials or data,
whose damage is often indirect and hard to quantify. 



\subsection{Untrusted Development}

High-stakes software is assembled from many influences: 
human
programmers, AI-generated code, libraries, documentation, 
build systems, and
review tools. Development becomes \emph{untrusted} when 
some of these cannot be
assumed benign. 
In this dissertation, we assume the human developers of high-stakes software
are well-intentioned: they do not deliberately inject harmful behavior
into the software they build. 
However, other tools, such as AI coding assistants, 
documentation, and review tools, may be untrusted
due to their black-box nature, 
their reliance on weakly trusted external content, 
and their potentially poisoned training data. 
An AI coding assistant may fabricate an API, a
documentation page may be poisoned, 
or a review tool may miss a subtle bug due 
to its prejudice toward common patterns.
The risk is then embedded in the artifact: once accepted, the deployed system will
faithfully execute it. 
Development-time defenses aim to catch or harden such
artifacts before they are trusted. In this dissertation, we
specifically instantiate
untrusted development as the use of \emph{AI-assisted coding
tools}: they
help implement a growing number of the code that reaches production, yet their
outputs carry no guarantee of being correct, benign, or free of
externally planted content.

\subsection{Untrusted Use}
\label{subsec:untrusted-use}

Even carefully written and audited software is only as secure as the way
it is used. Developers build for an expected usage model, for example,
who calls the system,
in what order, and at what scale. These 
assumptions may hold for ordinary users
yet fail against adversarial users, 
automated clients, or autonomous agents. 
Use becomes \emph{untrusted} when 
a deployed system is invoked by parties 
which are unknown to the developers at the 
time of deployment. 
Such misuse need not exploit any single bug: a harmful execution may
consist entirely of permitted operations arranged in an unexpected order or
scale (Section~\ref{subsec:example3}). Use-time defenses
aim to monitor and reject executions that fall outside acceptable behavior.
In this dissertation, In this dissertation, we
specifically instantiate untrusted use as the
execution of \emph{smart contracts}, programs deployed on a blockchain.
Smart contracts are an ideal instantiation of high-stakes systems facing 
untrusted use: they are high-stakes, since
they directly guard digital assets, and they are open to invocation by
any blockchain user, with no way to restrict who interacts with them.


Together, untrusted development and untrusted use are two forms of what
we call \emph{untrusted influence}: any actor, artifact, or interaction pattern
that shapes a high-stakes system without a sufficient basis for trust.
Development-time influence shapes what the software \emph{is}; use-time influence
shapes what it \emph{does}. 
This dissertation studies defenses at both points:
before deployment, by auditing and hardening the code that untrusted development
produces (Chapter \ref{part:development}), and after deployment, by detecting and blocking executions produced
under untrusted use (Chapter \ref{part:use}).
